Everyone knows la Mercè, the big September festival. Far fewer visitors know its little winter sister: the **Festes de Santa Eulàlia**, around **12 February**. For a few days, the old town brings back its giants, its human towers and its fire-spitting devils, and many museums open their doors for free. A more intimate festival, attended almost entirely by locals — and designed first and foremost for children.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Dates** | Around Friday 12 February 2027, usually through the following weekend |
| **Where** | The old town: Plaça de Sant Jaume, cathedral, La Rambla, the Born |
| **What's on** | Giants, human towers, correfoc, sardanas, concerts, free museums |
| **Price** | Free |
| **Crowd** | Families and locals: a very local feel |

## Who was Eulàlia?

Eulàlia was a **13-year-old** girl from Barcelona, martyred in the 4th century for refusing to renounce her Christian faith. Legend says she endured **thirteen tortures**, one for each year of her life. She is **co-patron saint of Barcelona**, alongside the Virgin of la Mercè, and rests in the crypt of the **cathedral**, which is dedicated to her.

Two traces of her to look for in the old town:
- **The 13 geese in the cathedral cloister** — they live around the cloister fountain, one for each year of the saint's life. Their whiteness symbolises her purity.
- **Baixada de Santa Eulàlia** — the steep little street in the Gothic Quarter where, legend has it, she was rolled down in a barrel full of broken glass. A statue of the saint watches over it from a niche.

People in Barcelona also say that **if it rains that day**, it's Eulàlia's tears.

## What not to miss

### The giants and the Laies

The city's **gegants** (giants) parade through the old town, surrounded by *capgrossos* (big heads). The queens of the festival are the **Laies**, the giantesses representing Eulàlia (*Laia* is her nickname), who gather for a big joint parade. During the festival, the giantesses are often displayed at the **Palau de la Virreina** on La Rambla, and the fire beasts and children's giants at the **Born CCM**.

### Human towers

The castellers build their **human towers** on Plaça de Sant Jaume, in front of City Hall and the Generalitat palace. It's the perfect chance to see castells up close, with far fewer people than at la Mercè.

### The correfoc

**Devils** run through the old town's streets in a shower of sparks. There's a **children's correfoc** in the late afternoon and an adults' one at night. To follow the latter up close: long-sleeved cotton clothes, a hood or cap, closed shoes — and nothing synthetic.

### The Àliga and the sardana

The **Àliga**, the city's eagle and one of the oldest figures of the festive procession, dances its **Protocols** before the city authorities. And in the squares, people dance the **sardana**, the Catalan circle dance: anyone can join in.

### Free museums

The festival's best deal: during the festes, **many museums and landmark buildings** open their doors for free (open-door days). The exact list is published by the city a few days before — we'll share it on our [Events](/en/evenements/) page. Go early, queues build fast.

## Practical tips

- **The 2027 programme** is published in early February on barcelona.cat/santaeulalia: castell and correfoc times change every year.
- **With kids**, stick to the daytime: giants, workshops, the children's correfoc. The night correfoc is more intense.
- **For the human towers**, arrive 30–45 minutes early on Plaça de Sant Jaume.
- **Free museums** get packed on Saturday: go at opening time, or on Sunday morning.
- **Santa Eulàlia isn't a public holiday** for shops: the city carries on as normal around the festival.
